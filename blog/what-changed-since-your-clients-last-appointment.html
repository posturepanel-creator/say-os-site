Beauty Passport

What changed since your client's last appointment?

You know your regular client. You remember their usual treatment and perhaps the products they prefer. But familiarity doesn't tell you everything that has happened since their last visit.

They may have started a medication, changed their skincare routine or had a treatment somewhere else. The useful question is simple: "Has anything changed since we last saw you?"

It's an easy question to skip. When someone has sat in your chair a dozen times, the consultation can shrink to a friendly catch-up. But the gap between appointments is exactly where things change — a new prescription, a course of acids started at home, a peel booked somewhere across town. None of it shows up in your memory of their last visit.

The changes that matter most are rarely dramatic. A client starts a retinoid in January and doesn't think to mention it in March. Someone books a quick peel before a holiday. A new supplement, a short course of antibiotics, a reaction to a product they tried once — small things on their side, but exactly the details that shape what you can safely do on yours. When the client keeps a record between visits, those details have somewhere to live, instead of depending on being remembered in a busy ten-minute catch-up.

That's the gap a client-held record is meant to close. In SAY, a client keeps their own Beauty Passport — allergies, skin notes, the products they use at home, treatments elsewhere — and chooses when to share it with you. When they do, the relevant information is there for you to review before you begin, rather than reconstructed from memory or a form filled in months ago.

A few things are worth being clear about. A record is only as current as the client has made it, so it supports your consultation — it doesn't replace the question. Sharing is the client's choice, and it doesn't hand every team member blanket access to everything. And SAY never decides whether a treatment is safe: it brings the information together; you make the professional judgement.

Illustrative example

A regular facial client updates their Beauty Passport with a new home skincare product before their next appointment. They share their record with the practitioner, who reviews it and asks any follow-up questions needed for the consultation.

That small habit — update, then share — turns "has anything changed?" from a question you hope to remember into something the client has often already answered before they arrive. It changes the tone of the consultation, too: instead of starting from a blank page, you start from what they've chosen to share, and spend your questions on what actually needs clarifying. If you run a salon in London, it's one of the ways SAY brings bookings, client records and business tools into one workspace.

Before the next appointment, ask your client to check that their information is current. Then review what they have chosen to share and record your consultation. SAY helps bring the information together; your professional assessment remains essential.
