# Can I refuse to treat a client?

Yes — but the reason has to be the treatment, not the person. UK salons are service providers under the Equality Act 2010, so a refusal that tracks a protected characteristic (disability, pregnancy, race, religion, age, gender reassignment, sex, sexual orientation) is discrimination, even when it feels like caution.

The difference in practice:

- **Defensible:** "I can't do this today because you've had sunbed exposure in the last 48 hours." Clinical, time-limited, documented, applies to everyone.
- **Not defensible:** "We don't treat clients with HIV." That is a disability under the Act, and standard precautions already cover it. The same logic catches blanket bans on epilepsy, dementia, or wheelchair users.

What keeps you on the right side of it:

- **Refuse the treatment, offer an alternative.** Declining a chemical peel on active eczema but offering a hydrating facial is an adjustment, not a refusal. The Act expects reasonable adjustments, not perfect ones.
- **Write down the clinical reason at the time**, in the client's record — not just "declined."
- **Apply the rule consistently.** A contraindication you enforce for one client and waive for another is evidence against you.
- **Behaviour is separate.** You can refuse service for abuse, intoxication, or threats to staff, and you should. Keep that policy written and applied to everyone.

Conditions listed in your insurer's contraindication schedule are usually a requirement to consult or get GP consent — not permission to decline outright. Read what yours actually says before you rely on it.
