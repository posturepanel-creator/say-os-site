# Can I do a facial or peel on a client with active acne?

Usually yes, but the treatment you choose depends on what kind of acne it is and what the client is putting on or taking for it. Acne is not a blanket contraindication; the wrong facial on the wrong skin is.

**Mild to moderate acne (blackheads, whiteheads, some papules)** is fine to treat. Gentle cleansing, enzyme exfoliation, LED and light salicylic peels (up to about 2%) all sit comfortably within a beauty-therapy scope. Skip steam and heavy oils on congested, oily skin.

**Inflamed, cystic or nodular acne** needs a lighter touch. Do not extract cysts or nodules, do not do microdermabrasion or microneedling over active lesions, and avoid anything abrasive. Extractions on inflamed skin spread bacteria and risk scarring and post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation, which is harder to treat than the acne itself. Refer to their GP if they are not already under one — cystic acne is a medical condition.

**Check what they are using before you touch the skin:**

- Topical retinoids (adapalene, tretinoin) or benzoyl peroxide: skin is thinner and more reactive. No waxing, no peels above superficial strength, and a patch test for anything active.
- Oral antibiotics (lymecycline, doxycycline): photosensitising. Avoid IPL, laser and sun-exposing treatments; keep peels superficial.
- Roaccutane (isotretinoin), current or within the last six months: no peels, no waxing, no microneedling, no dermabrasion. Full stop.

**Record it.** Note the acne type, what they use, and what you did and did not do. If a client has a reaction after a facial, that note is what protects you.

**One rule of thumb:** if the skin is red, hot, weeping or painful today, reschedule. A calm skin next week is a better outcome than an angry one tomorrow.
