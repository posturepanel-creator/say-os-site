# Can I treat a client with hepatitis B, hepatitis C or HIV?

Yes. And in most cases, turning them away would be unlawful.

Under the **Equality Act 2010**, HIV counts as a disability from the point of diagnosis, and hepatitis B or C usually will too where it has a long-term substantial effect. Refusing a standard treatment purely because a client discloses a blood-borne virus is direct discrimination — the same as refusing on grounds of race or pregnancy. "We'll book you last thing on a Friday" is not a workaround; segregating a client by condition is also discrimination.

The clinical answer is simpler than most therapists expect: **your infection control should already be at the level required.** Universal precautions assume every client may carry a blood-borne virus, because most people who do are undiagnosed. If your practice is only safe when a client discloses, it isn't safe.

What that means in practice:

- **Single-use anything that breaks skin.** Needles, blades, microneedling cartridges, lancets — used once, straight into a sharps bin. Never re-sterilised.
- **Autoclave your reusable metal tools.** Wipes and UV cabinets are not sterilisation. Local authority licensing for skin piercing, electrolysis and tattooing (Local Government (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act 1982) will expect an autoclave with a log.
- **Cover your own cuts** with a waterproof dressing, and wear gloves for any treatment with a realistic bleed risk.
- **Get vaccinated against hepatitis B.** It is the single highest-value thing you can do for your own protection, and it's free from most GPs for at-risk occupations.
- **Know your sharps injury protocol** before you need it: bleed it, wash it, don't scrub, and go to A&E or occupational health the same day. PEP for HIV works best within hours.

Where a genuine clinical judgement does apply, it is about the client's current health, not the diagnosis label — advanced liver disease can mean easy bruising and impaired clotting, which is relevant to waxing, microneedling and deep tissue massage. Ask about bruising and bleeding, not about their status.

You are also not entitled to demand a client's status. Disclosure is voluntary. A consultation form should ask about bleeding, bruising, healing and medication, and hold whatever is disclosed as special category health data under UK GDPR — accessible only to the therapist who needs it, never gossiped across the salon floor.
