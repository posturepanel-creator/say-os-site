# Do I need my own insurance if I rent a chair in a salon in the UK?

Yes, in practice — and the salon owner's policy almost certainly doesn't cover you.

A genuine chair renter is self-employed and runs their own business inside someone else's premises. The salon's public liability policy covers the salon's own liability as the business. Most policies exclude independent contractors unless they are named on it, so if your client is injured or their colour damages a coat, you may be personally exposed.

**Public liability** is not compulsory by law for a self-employed stylist. But it's effectively required: most salon owners will insist on a certificate before you start, and many council special treatment licences (for example for microneedling or semi-permanent make-up) require it as a condition.

**Professional indemnity / treatment risk** is what actually responds to a claim that your service caused harm, such as an allergic reaction or burn. Public liability alone often won't pay for that. Check that your policy lists every treatment you offer, because an unlisted treatment is an uncovered treatment.

**Employers' liability** is legally required the moment you employ anyone, including an assistant or apprentice, even part-time. A sole renter with no staff doesn't need it.

Before signing a rental agreement, check:

- **Cover limit:** £1m is the common minimum landlords ask for; £2m–£5m is often available cheaply.
- **Named treatments:** your exact services, including any product or patch-test requirements the insurer sets.
- **Salon owner's wording:** ask whether they require you to be named or to name them as an interested party.
- **Certificate on file:** keep a current copy and renewal date, since a lapsed policy voids the protection.

If you're unsure whether you're truly self-employed, ask your insurer and an accountant before you sign, because HMRC looks at the substance of the arrangement, not the label.
